Method of Hypothesis — Critical Notes¶

Geometric Reconstruction & Academic Hypothesis · Meno 86e–87b

I. Geometric Significance¶

Socrates’ Elliptic Application Hypothesis (86e–87b): As Sir Thomas Heath demonstrated (History of Greek Mathematics, Vol. I, pp. 298–303), Socrates’ famous geometric obscurity at 87a—inscribing a given area \(C\) as a triangle in a circle of diameter \(AB = d\)—reduces to choosing a point \(X\) on diameter \(AB\) and erecting ordinate \(XY\) to the circle so that rectangle \(AX \cdot XY = C\). Since in the semicircle \(XY^2 = AX \cdot XB\), squaring gives \(AX \cdot XB = (C / AX)^2\), meaning rectangle \(AX \cdot XY\) is applied (parateinein) along \(AB\) while falling short (ἐλλείπειν) on \(XB\) by a rectangle \(XB \cdot XY\) similar to the applied rectangle \(AX \cdot XY\) (\(AX : XY = XY : XB\))—an exact fourth-century instance of hyperbolic/elliptic application of areas (Elements VI.28).

II. Literal Greek Mathematical Philology (Contra Paraphrasim)¶

  • 87a3–6 — παρὰ τὴν δοθεῖσαν αὐτοῦ γραμμὴν παρατείναντα ἐλλείπειν τοιούτῳ χωρίῳ οἷον ἂν αὐτὸ τὸ παρατεταμένον ᾖ: Jowett renders loosely ‘whether, if the figure be applied to the given line of the circle, it is deficient by another figure similar to that which is applied’. Literally, παρατείνειν παρὰ γραμμήν is the technical Pythagorean-Academic idiom for parabolic/elliptic application of an area along a given straight line (παραβολή / ἔλλειψις τῶν χωρίων), later standardized by Euclid in Book VI and by Apollonius in naming the parabola and ellipse.

III. Reception¶

  • Euclid (Elements VI.28): Generalizes the elliptic application of area to arbitrary rectilineal figures.

  • Proclus (In Euclidem, Friedlein 45.15–46.3): Emphasizes that hypothesis is the supreme engine of Academic geometric reduction.
